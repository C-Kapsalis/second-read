# Journalism and reported nonfiction: accuracy before elegance

Use for reported articles, interviews, investigative/explanatory features, news analysis and data journalism. A first-person opinion column should also load `POLICY-AND-ARGUMENTS.md` but is not the same thing as a reported news item. The journalist/editor's named publication guide outranks a generic style guide within its scope.

## Three independent layers

1. **Reporting integrity:** identifiable source, quotation fidelity, time/place, denominator, provenance of data, fair characterization of contested statements, known uncertainty, corrections. An invented quote or source is not an allowable stylistic change.
2. **Editorial structure:** accurate headline and lead, essential who/what/when/where/why/how as relevant, clear attribution before surprising claims, distinction between verified fact and analysis, reader knowledge progression. Dramatic openings are acceptable if context arrives before readers are misled.
3. **House style:** spelling, abbreviation, numerals, titles, dates and punctuation. Apply the publication's own guide when provided.

The University of Oxford Style Guide (https://www.ox.ac.uk/about/the-university/brand/style-guide) is **an Oxford University institutional house guide**, not a universal newsroom code. Use it only when the writer chooses Oxford conventions or publishes on behalf of Oxford. Its page explicitly distinguishes itself from commercial Oxford University Press writing guides. Do not claim that “Oxford style” settles evidence ethics.

## Source and quote decisions

- Every checkable headline claim must be traced to a source. A quoted official's assertion is still **an assertion by that source**, not independent proof.
- Distinguish event date from publication date, before/after comparisons, changes in definitions and population denominators.
- When data are provisional, outdated or incomplete, keep that qualifier near the finding, not in a distant footnote.
- Do not change the substance of direct quotations for elegance. Suggest paraphrase and correct attribution instead, while distinguishing it from exact quotation.
- A meaningful missing perspective may justify a reporting question, **not fabricated rebuttal**.
- For politically contested material, present documented claims, evidence, and counterarguments without assigning partisan rankings or optimizing voters' emotions.

## When a visual would genuinely help

Load `DATA-STORYTELLING.md` if the piece asks readers to compare many numbers, see a temporal trend, understand a distribution or follow a causal/organizational relationship. Propose a visual *with data requirements and alt-text plan*, not an ungrounded chart. If the source data are missing, recommend collecting/validating them before visualizing.

## Paired judgments

**Edit — attribution inflation**
- Original: “The policy will save £20 million, experts agree.” Only source is a department's unverified forecast.
- Suggested: “The department forecasts £20 million in savings,” followed by methodology and independent assessment if available. Do not invent expert consensus.

**Leave — precise, attributed statement**
- “The survey interviewed 812 adults online between 2 and 9 April.” If supplied methodology matches, do not generalize to the whole population without weighting and sampling context.

**Edit — misleading time comparison**
- Chart/caption compares 2022 gross totals with 2025 per-capita rates as a single trend. Flag incompatible units and request a consistent series; do not invent missing values.

**Leave — a memorable reported scene**
- A verified account starts with a vivid witness description and supplies source/time/context before making a contested general claim. Do not strip a vivid detail merely because it isn't a headline fact.

## Questions at point of reading

“After this number, does the reader know **whose measurement** this is, for **which population and period**?” Link any question to a proposed attribution or qualification, not to a judgement of the author.
