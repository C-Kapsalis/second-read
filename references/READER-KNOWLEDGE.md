# Reader knowledge: information unfolds in time

Model what a first-time reader can understand **at the point they encounter each passage**, not using facts from later in the piece. Connect this analysis to source-anchored editing proposals. Do not demand a summary, mind map, or quiz unless it changes a meaningful editorial decision.

## Minimal internal reading ledger

For each important block or thread, track:

- **Introduced:** which people, terms, claims, examples and choices appear now?
- **Established:** what is actually supported by the text and supplied evidence so far?
- **Expected:** what is the reader invited to expect next (an answer, objection, decision, proof)?
- **Unresolved:** which pronouns, concepts, conditions or questions remain open?
- **Effect:** what can the reader plausibly feel or infer, with uncertainty rather than invented psychological fact?
- **Next:** does the following section answer, delay, contradict, or intentionally subvert that expectation?

When an edit is needed, propose a concrete operation and then run the ledger again with the new order.

## Common flow defects and interventions

| Observable pattern | Proposed edit | Leave alone if |
|---|---|---|
| Conclusion before indispensable definition | Move a definition or add a five-word bridge | Hook deliberately creates curiosity and quickly pays it off |
| Example without interpretive point | Add the minimum connecting sentence after it | Narrative allows example to resonate without a moral |
| Abstract claim before reader has a foothold | Move supplied concrete example earlier | Specialist audience knows the concept already |
| Qualifications arrive after irreversible impression | Move qualification alongside claim | Intentional suspense is disclosed at a safe moment |
| Repeated claim in distant paragraphs | Merge or trim one occurrence | Refrain is purposeful and gains new meaning |
| Counterargument introduced after declaring certainty | Qualify claim or move objection before resolution | Writer deliberately stages a dramatic rebuttal accurately |

## Questions in the original text

When a passage could materially mislead a first-time reader, put **“Questions to yourself after reading this once”** immediately after the verbatim excerpt, in source order, with an anchor to the corresponding proposal. Prefer one or two; maximum three. A question must be answerable from the original content at that stage and must not smuggle in future information as if already known.

A context-aware question for a software quickstart: “At this step, do I know where the API key comes from?” If the key's source is not in the supplied product material, ask to verify; do not invent setup instructions.

## Example

- P1: “Free fares are the solution.” Reader expects a fare-policy defense.
- P2: “Bus reliability may be the bigger problem.” Reader now expects a reliability argument.
- P3: “We should pilot reliability for six months.” Reader expects a limited trial.
- P4: “That's why free fares must begin immediately.” Reader perceives a causal gap.

If the brief says pilot-first, propose exact P1/P4 replacements while retaining an effective sentence in P2. If the brief does not establish the position, do not choose one just to smooth P4.
