# Brand, audience and persuasion

Load this when a fix depends on the brand's personality, the reader's stage,
or a persuasion lever. Every piece of copy either strengthens the brand or
dilutes it; this file says how to tell which, with a quote.

## Readers are cognitive misers

People spend as little mental effort as they can. Copy that makes them work
gets abandoned, however good it is: confusion is one of the most common
reasons a purchase stops. So: clear hierarchy, scannable blocks, the most
important thing first, detail later (progressive disclosure).

## What kind of purchase is this?

| Type | What drives it | What the copy needs |
|---|---|---|
| Utilitarian | Function and price | Features, specs, comparisons, value |
| Low-involvement | Habit, little risk | Brand recognition and availability; long persuasion will not be read |
| Ego-intensive | Identity, experience, status | Aspiration, lifestyle, belonging, story |

A long feature essay for a low-involvement product, or a spec sheet for an
identity purchase, is a stage-fit problem (U8).

## Brand personality (five dimensions)

| Dimension | Sounds like | Check the copy for | Breaks when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sincerity | Down to earth, honest, warm, cheerful | Plain words, real customers, transparency, community | Corporate jargon, hype, pressure, insults |
| Excitement | Daring, spirited, imaginative, current | Energy, novelty, bold ideas, shareable surprise | Dull filler, stale references, gimmicks with no substance |
| Competence | Reliable, intelligent, successful | Precise language, evidence, sources, credentials | Unsupported claims, sloppy errors, vague results |
| Sophistication | Refined, charming, exclusive | Restraint, elevated words, quality signals | Shouting, discount-bin urgency, exclamation marks, mass-market slang |
| Ruggedness | Outdoorsy, tough, direct | Durability, real-world proof, plain talk | Soft, urbane, performative toughness |

Each has a risk to watch: sincerity can read naive, excitement gimmicky,
competence cold, sophistication narrow, ruggedness performative.

## Archetypes

One archetype per brand, kept across every piece: Innocent (simplicity,
goodness), Explorer (freedom, discovery), Sage (wisdom, truth), Hero
(courage, achievement), Outlaw (breaking rules), Magician (transformation),
Regular Person (belonging, accessibility), Lover (passion, intimacy), Jester
(joy, humour), Caregiver (care, protection), Creator (imagination,
expression), Ruler (control, status). A Caregiver bakery that taunts its
followers, or a Sage brand that shouts, is archetype confusion: flag it with
both quotes (U9).

## Segments, personas and positioning

- Copy written for everyone resonates with no one. Name the persona: goals,
  pain points, objections, where they read. If the user gives one, judge the
  copy against it.
- Positioning: for [audience], [brand] is the [category] that [point of
  difference], because [reason to believe]. Check that the copy carries the
  point of difference and gives the reason to believe; a claimed difference
  with no reason to believe is a proof problem (U5).
- One-sentence strategy test: we will reach [objective] with [content] that
  makes [audience] feel [emotion] so they can [outcome]. If you cannot fill it
  from the piece, the piece has no single message (U3).

## Lifecycle stage

| Stage | Content that fits | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Education, ideas, entertainment, search-friendly answers | Useful without knowing the brand; no premature selling; easy to share |
| Consideration | Case studies, comparisons, demos, expert content | Covers the reader's evaluation criteria, handles objections, shows the difference |
| Decision | Testimonials, guarantees, pricing, implementation help | Makes acting easy, reduces risk, urgency without manipulation, all practical facts present |
| Retention | Onboarding, usage tips, member offers, community | Adds value to what they bought, recognises them, upsells without eroding trust |
| Advocacy | Referral programmes, user content, co-creation | Makes sharing easy, rewards and credits advocates |

## Persuasion levers: honest vs not

| Lever | Honest use | Flag when |
|---|---|---|
| Framing | Gains for motivation; losses for risk-averse or compliance messages | Loss framing that becomes guilt, shame or threat |
| Anchoring | A real previous price or a premium option shown first | The reference price never existed, or the "was" price is wrong |
| Scarcity and urgency | A real date, a real quantity, and the reason | "Ends soon", "almost gone", a countdown that resets or contradicts another date |
| Social proof | Specific: count, named customer, result, source | Anonymous quotes, invented-sounding numbers, ratings with no count |
| Authority | A named, relevant expert or credential | "Experts agree", "clinically proven" with no study |
| Reciprocity | Free value before the ask | A "gift" that needs a purchase the copy hides |
| Defaults | A sensible pre-selected option the reader can change | Pre-ticked marketing consent, add-ons pre-added to the cart |
| Choice | 3 or fewer options, one recommended | Many equal options and no guidance (choice overload) |
| Mental accounting | Placing a premium item in an "experience" or "investment" budget | Hiding the total cost |
| Availability | A vivid, memorable example | A vivid example that misrepresents the typical result |

## Website and search

The website is the brand's main owned channel: navigation should follow how
visitors think, not the org chart; pages should load fast and work on a
phone. Landing pages exist to give one campaign one focused page. In search,
choose keywords that fit the brand: ranking for an off-brand term brings
traffic that dilutes the brand or expects something the brand cannot give.
Detailed on-page SEO checks are in landing-page.md (L15-L24) and blog.md
(B2, B3, B5, B6, B14).

## Channels

Owned (site, blog, email), earned (shares, press, organic search) and paid
(ads, sponsored posts) each do a different job; paid seeds earned and both
should lead to owned. Copy must be native to its platform: format, length and
style conventions differ, so the same text pasted everywhere fails somewhere
(social.md has the limits).

## Quality over volume

Each piece should earn its place with real information, entertainment or
utility that readers would seek out. Stories should show values through
action, not assert them. Mediocre content costs money and erodes the brand.

## AI-written copy

AI drafts are fine; homogenised, generic copy is not. The tells are template
phrasing, generic superlatives, uniform paragraph lengths, and no specific
detail (U10). Second Read is a pre-publication check, not the final word: a
human approves high-stakes copy, owns the brand voice, and handles exceptions.

## Brand risks to catch before publishing

- **Personality inconsistency:** the piece sounds like a different brand.
- **Quality slip:** errors and filler from production pressure.
- **Channel misfit:** the brand somewhere its audience does not expect it.
- **Message contradiction:** this piece contradicts another claim or value.
- **Cultural missteps:** wording that could offend the audience or the people
  next to it; flag it and suggest a second reviewer.
- **Competitor attacks:** disparaging a competitor hurts the brand more than
  the competitor, and an unsourced claim about them is a Blocker (U5).
- **Crisis tone:** in bad news, sincere brands lead with honesty and
  accountability; competent brands lead with the fix.
